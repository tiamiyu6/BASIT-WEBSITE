# HOW DID HUMANS INVENT THE CELL PHONE?: script (our own words, same title and structure as the studied video)

Format: bold-outline cartoon explainer, second-person opening, 8 chapters, fast cuts. About 10 minutes.

**Education only. Every claim below is hedged where sources differ. Check the facts table before posting.**

---

1. **It is six in the morning in Lagos. Your alarm rings, and you reach out without opening your eyes.**
2. **Before you even stand up, you check your messages, the weather and the football scores.**
3. **You use this thing many times a day, and you hardly think about it. But look at it for a second.**
4. **It talks to people on the other side of the world. It is a camera, a map, a bank and a torch.**
5. **And here is the strange part. Everything inside it was dug out of the ground. Literally.**
6. **Reportedly, more than sixty different elements are packed in there. Metals, sand, even the glass of the screen.**
7. **So how did humans invent the cell phone? Let's find out.**

## CHAPTER 1: THE METALS
8. **Let's start with the oldest ingredient, copper.**
9. **Around seven thousand years ago, people began melting copper out of rocks. It was one of the first metals humans learned to work.**
10. **Copper carries electricity better than almost any cheap metal. So today it runs through every wire in your phone.**
11. **Then there is gold. It is soft and it never rusts, so a thin layer protects the tiny connectors inside.**
12. **Mix copper with tin, and you get bronze. That helped start a whole new age, about five thousand years ago.**
13. **Tin did not go away. Even now, a little tin is used to join the parts inside your phone.**
14. **Remember copper and tin. They will come back.**

## CHAPTER 2: SAND AND GLASS
15. **Now picture a beach.**
16. **About three and a half thousand years ago, craftspeople in the Middle East and Egypt learned to melt sand into glass.**
17. **Early glass was cloudy and rare. But they found that adding a little cobalt turned it a deep, beautiful blue.**
18. **Remember cobalt. That blue mineral has a big role to play in your phone.**
19. **Over the centuries, glass got flatter, clearer and stronger, until it could cover a screen.**
20. **Hold that thought. We will come back to the screen. First, we need a spark.**

## CHAPTER 3: THE SPARK
21. **Long before phones, people noticed odd things in nature.**
22. **A rock called lodestone pulled iron towards it, and rubbed amber made small feathers jump.**
23. **Nobody knew why. It looked like magic. But those clues pointed to a hidden force: electricity.**
24. **In 1800, an Italian scientist called Alessandro Volta stacked metal discs and made the first battery.**
25. **For the first time, humans had a steady flow of electricity they could control.**
26. **Then came the telegraph. Dots and dashes could race along a wire, and messages crossed countries in seconds.**
27. **In 1876, Alexander Graham Bell patented a way to send actual voices down a wire. The telephone was born.**
28. **The first words are reported to have been something like, 'Mr. Watson, come here. I want to see you.'**
29. **But there was a problem. A voice could only travel where a wire was already laid.**

## CHAPTER 4: INVISIBLE WAVES
30. **What if the message could fly without a wire?**
31. **In the 1860s, a Scottish physicist, James Clerk Maxwell, worked out on paper that light and electricity are connected, and that invisible electric waves must exist.**
32. **In 1887, a German scientist, Heinrich Hertz, proved it. He sent a spark across a room and caught it with a small loop of wire.**
33. **Those invisible waves are what we now call radio waves. Hertz even gave his name to the way we measure them.**
34. **Then Guglielmo Marconi turned them into a message machine. By 1901, he reported a signal sent across the Atlantic Ocean.**
35. **Now voice could travel with no wire at all. But radios were big, and they only worked one way at a time.**

## CHAPTER 5: THE BRAIN
36. **A phone needs more than a voice. It needs a brain.**
37. **Early electronics used glass tubes called vacuum tubes. They were big, hot and burned out all the time.**
38. **Then, in 1947, three scientists at Bell Labs, John Bardeen, Walter Brattain and William Shockley, built the transistor.**
39. **A transistor is a tiny switch with no moving parts. Turn it on, turn it off. That is the language of every computer.**
40. **Even better, it could be made from silicon, and silicon is made from ordinary sand.**
41. **So the beach from chapter two came back. Sand, cleaned and melted into near-perfect crystal.**
42. **In the late 1950s, Jack Kilby and Robert Noyce found how to put many transistors on one chip.**
43. **Today, a chip the size of a fingernail can hold billions of transistors. That is the brain in your pocket.**

## CHAPTER 6: THE CELL
44. **Now we have a voice, a wire-free wave, and a brain. How do we connect millions of people at once?**
45. **Early car phones used one giant tower for a whole city, so only a few people could talk at once.**
46. **Then Bell Labs engineers had an idea in the late 1940s: split the city into small areas called cells, each with its own tower.**
47. **As you move, your call is passed from one cell to the next, like a baton in a relay race.**
48. **Each cell can reuse the same radio channels as far-away cells. That is how millions of people can talk at the same time.**
49. **Then on 3 April 1973, a Motorola engineer called Martin Cooper stood on a New York street and made the first public call from a handheld mobile phone.**
50. **According to the story he tells, he called a rival engineer at Bell Labs to say, 'I'm calling you from a real cell phone.'**
51. **That first phone weighed about a kilogram. You could talk for roughly half an hour, then charge it for about ten hours.**
52. **In 1983, the first commercial version went on sale for about four thousand dollars. Only the rich could own one.**
53. **Since then, phones have shrunk year after year, as chips got smaller and batteries got stronger.**

## CHAPTER 7: POWER AND GLASS
54. **A phone that dies in an hour is not much use. It needed a better battery.**
55. **In the 1970s, British chemist Stanley Whittingham found a way to store energy in lithium. John Goodenough later made it far more powerful.**
56. **And that blue mineral from the glass maker? Cobalt. It was a key part of the new battery.**
57. **In 1991, Sony brought the lithium-ion battery to market, and Akira Yoshino's safer design helped make it practical.**
58. **But there is a cost. Much of the world's cobalt is mined in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, and there are serious reports of dangerous work and child labour.**
59. **That is why many companies are now working on tracing cobalt and on batteries that use less of it.**
60. **Now the screen. The glass is often strengthened by dipping it in a hot bath of potassium salt, so the surface can resist cracks.**
61. **Behind the glass sits a display. In 1888, an Austrian botanist, Friedrich Reinitzer, noticed a carrot chemical that melted in two stages and turned cloudy before turning clear.**
62. **That strange state is a liquid crystal. Control it with electricity, and you can control light.**
63. **Finally, your fingers. A clear coating made from indium and tin senses your touch, because your finger carries a tiny electric charge.**
64. **And there is the tin again, just as I promised.**

## CHAPTER 8: THE POCKET
65. **Put it all together and you get a smartphone.**
66. **The IBM Simon, in the early 1990s, was an early attempt. In 2007, a touchscreen phone changed the game for everyone.**
67. **Now think about Nigeria. Before 2001, there were very few phone lines, and reportedly fewer than half a million working ones for a very large country.**
68. **In January 2001, Nigeria held an auction for mobile licences. By August 2001, GSM service had started.**
69. **Since then, mobile phones have spread across the country. Today, well over a hundred million lines are reported in use.**
70. **For millions of people, the phone became their bank, their shop, their classroom and their link to family far away.**
71. **Think of the chain. Rocks from the ground. Sand from the beach. Waves in the air. Thousands of minds, over thousands of years.**
72. **Back to your alarm. That little rectangle was not invented by one person on one day.**
73. **It is a pocket full of human history, dug up, melted, grown from sand and stitched together by people you will never meet.**
74. **And that is the story of the cell phone.**
75. **Next time, we trace another thing in your pocket: the small piece of plastic that lets you pay without cash. How did humans invent the credit card?**
76. **If this surprised you, tell us in the comments which part you did not know, and what we should trace next.**
