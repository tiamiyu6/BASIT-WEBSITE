# HOW DID A BOY SELLING SWEETS BECOME AFRICA'S RICHEST MAN? (Dangote remake, Engine format): script (draft 2)

Format: second-person cold open, 8 chapters, one picture per line, 3 to 4 cuts per picture, no burned-in captions.

**Education only. Cartoon characters, not portraits. Every claim is hedged ('reportedly', 'according to reports'). No net-worth figure.**

---

## COLD OPEN
1. **It is a hot afternoon in Kano, and a small boy is selling sweets to other children.**
2. **He counts every coin twice. He doesn't know it yet, but he is practising for something enormous.**
3. **Now jump forward many years. That same boy is standing in front of a refinery that can process six hundred and fifty thousand barrels of crude oil a day.**
4. **His name is Aliko Dangote, and Forbes has listed him as the richest person in Africa for many years.**
5. **You might think this story is about luck, or knowing the right people. But the pattern underneath it is much simpler.**
6. **In the next few minutes, we'll follow six big ideas through his story, and show how each one can work for a small business too.**
7. **So how did a boy who sold sweets build one of Africa's biggest business groups? Let's find out.**

## CHAPTER 1: THE TRADER'S FAMILY
8. **Aliko Dangote was born in 1957, in Kano, in northern Nigeria.**
9. **Kano has been a trading city for centuries. Camel caravans crossed the desert to its markets.**
10. **His family was part of that world. His great-grandfather, Alhassan Dantata, was a well-known merchant in West Africa.**
11. **So business wasn't something he learned in a classroom. It was the dinner-table conversation.**
12. **As a boy, he reportedly bought sweets in bulk and sold them to other children for a profit.**
13. **That is idea number one: buy for less, sell for more. Every business on earth is a bigger version of that.**
14. **He went to school in Nigeria, then studied business at Al-Azhar University in Cairo.**
15. **Seeing another country showed him how other markets worked. And then he came home with a plan.**

## CHAPTER 2: THE LOAN
16. **In 1977, he moved to Lagos. Lagos was loud, crowded and full of chances.**
17. **He had ideas, but not much money. So he asked a family member for help.**
18. **According to widely reported accounts, his uncle lent him about five hundred thousand naira.**
19. **That sounds small today, but it was the seed money for everything that followed.**
20. **With it, he started trading basic goods that every household and builder needs: rice, sugar and cement.**
21. **Notice what he chose. Not a fancy product that only some people want. Things everybody needs, every week.**
22. **In 1981, he set up the company that would grow into the Dangote Group.**
23. **Lesson one: start with something people always need, even if you start small.**

## CHAPTER 3: THE LEMON PROBLEM
24. **Now here is where the story gets clever. Imagine you sell lemonade.**
25. **But you buy your lemons from someone else.**
26. **If he raises the price, your lemonade gets expensive. If he stops selling, your business stops.**
27. **So what if you grew the lemons yourself?**
28. **Economists call that backward integration: making the things you used to buy.**
29. **In the early 2000s, the Nigerian government began pushing companies to make more goods at home instead of importing them.**
30. **Dangote saw the moment. His group began moving from trading into manufacturing.**
31. **Sugar. Flour. Salt. And the big one: cement.**
32. **Making the product means you keep more of the profit, and you're less at the mercy of other people's prices.**
33. **Lesson two: don't only buy and sell. Look for the step you can own.**

## CHAPTER 4: THE CEMENT KINGDOM
34. **For years, Nigeria imported a big share of its cement.**
35. **That meant ships, delays, and prices that jumped around.**
36. **The group built a huge cement plant at Obajana in Kogi State, which began operating in 2007.**
37. **A cement plant is a giant: limestone is dug from the ground, crushed, heated in a kiln and ground into powder.**
38. **Every road, bridge and house needs it, so demand never stops.**
39. **In 2010, Dangote Cement was listed on the Nigerian stock exchange, so ordinary investors could own a share.**
40. **Later, the company spread to other African countries too.**
41. **More cement made in Africa means fewer imports, more local jobs, and more money staying on the continent.**
42. **Lesson three: pick a business where demand is steady, then get big enough to serve it well.**

## CHAPTER 5: SUGAR, SALT AND FLOUR
43. **Cement wasn't the only thing. The group also built sugar refineries, salt plants and flour mills.**
44. **Think about your own kitchen. Sugar in your tea, salt in your stew, flour in your bread.**
45. **A business that sells to every kitchen and every building site has customers on every street.**
46. **But when one company grows this large, people start asking harder questions about competition and prices.**
47. **We'll come back to that.**

## CHAPTER 6: THE GIANT BET
48. **Then Dangote chose a much bigger target.**
49. **Nigeria is one of Africa's biggest oil producers. But for years, it sold crude oil abroad and imported much of its petrol and diesel.**
50. **Think about that. Sell the raw lemons cheap, and buy the lemonade back at a high price.**
51. **So the group decided to build its own refinery, in the Lekki area near Lagos.**
52. **It was designed to process six hundred and fifty thousand barrels of crude oil a day.**
53. **It reportedly cost around twenty billion dollars.**
54. **It also took much longer than planned, with delays and disputes along the way.**
55. **Many people doubted it would ever work.**
56. **In 2024, the refinery began producing diesel and petrol, according to reports.**
57. **Lesson four: very big plans take a very long time. Patience is part of the price.**

## CHAPTER 7: HERO OR VILLAIN?
58. **Now, a fair story has to show both sides.**
59. **Many people admire the group for building factories, creating jobs, and making goods inside Nigeria.**
60. **Supporters say that if Nigeria makes more of what it uses, money and skills stay here.**
61. **But critics say that when one group becomes this big, it can have too much power over prices and competition.**
62. **They also say big projects can be helped by government policies that smaller companies don't get.**
63. **Both of these views are part of the real story.**
64. **And it is a useful reminder: success on this scale brings attention, and questions, too.**

## CHAPTER 8: THE RULES
65. **So what can an ordinary person learn from all this?**
66. **One: start small, with something people always need.**
67. **Two: don't only buy and sell. Look for a step you can own.**
68. **Three: understand your whole chain, from raw materials to the customer.**
69. **Four: think in years, not weeks.**
70. **Five: big bets carry big risks, so never gamble money you can't afford to lose.**
71. **Six: build trust, because trade runs on people believing you will deliver.**
72. **You don't need billions to use these ideas. You can use them on a small shop, a skill, or a side business.**

## ENDING
73. **From sweets in Kano to a refinery in Lagos.**
74. **The tools were simple: buy for less, sell for more, own the next step, and be patient.**
75. **Which of these six rules will you use first? Tell us in the comments.**
